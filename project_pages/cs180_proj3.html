Part A.3: Warp the Images

This project part called for me to apply inverse warping to my images through 2 different interpolation methods: Nearest-Neighbor, and Bilinearly.

Nearest-Neighbor interpolation involved filling in our warped pixels by rounding our coordinate to the nearest pixel value. Using this sort of interpolation was fast, (in comparison to bilinear interpolation) however, if you examine the warped images below, the warped results are more 'jagged'. Take the 'NN-Warped Couch Pt.2' as an example, you can see how the plushies have a more low-resolution appearance, and the shape of them is a bit more dramatic than in the bilinear example we'll see later on.

Bilinear interpolation involves using the weighted average 4 nearby pixels to fill in our warped pixels. I took the average of the 4 corner pixels for my bilinear implementation. This function ran a bit slower than the NN interpolation, however the results were a more smooth appearance to the warped images. Intuitively, it makes sense that this implementation would result in a longer runtime and smoother appearance, since the averaging would increase the runtime, but result in more sophisticated pixel values than simply rounding.

I also had to rectify my photos, which means to transform a rectangular region of my original photo, one which isn't perfectly rectangular due to skewing/angles, and use homography to make the photo properly rectangular.

For my couch photos, I chose to rectify the rectangular couch cushions. They're a bit hidden by pillows in the photo, but the red Xs in the image below shows the 4 corners of the cushion.

For my first street photo, I rectified a small, rectangular window near the top of my photo. I rectified the car door in my second street photo.

Part A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Finally, it is time to blend our image sets together so that they form cohesive mosaics! My mosaic-ing function took both images, and their H matrix as inputs, and went about blending through these steps:

  1. Project the corners of image2 onto the corners of image1 in order to translate the bounds of image2 to image1's frame
  2. Obtain the minimum and maximum X and Y coordinate values amongst the corners of image1, and projected image2.
  3. Use the differences between the maximum and minimum values to obtain the final mosaic's dimensions. Create our mosaic image, and weight matrix using the dimensions. Fill the matrices with zeroes for now.
  4. Copy image1 into our mosaic image, while factoring in the offset created by the mosaic origin. We can just copy image1 directly because we will be warping image2 to fit image1. As image1 pixels are being copied to the mosaic, also add +1 to the corresponding pixel in our weight matrix.
  5. Warp image2 (I used my bilinear interpolaton function to do this). When warping, instead of using the prior H matrix, we multiplied the H matrix by an offset matrix so that the coordinates of the warped image2 won't be in any negative quadrants.
  6. Add our warped image2 pixels to the mosaic matrix, and increment the corresponding weight pixels.
  7. Normalize our mosaic by dividing the RGB values of our non-zero pixels by the corresponding weight value in the weight matrix. The weight value indicates how many images are been layered at the corresponding pixel. This will prevent our overlapping regions from being too bright.

Part B.4: RANSAC for Robust Homography

Below are the side-by-side comparisons between my automatic mosaics, whose H matrix was created with the help of a Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) homography function, and my manual mosaic from Part A.4 of this project. I didn't see too much of an observable difference between my street mosaics, which may be due to the fact that my street images share a lot of unique features and objects (ie. cars, leaves, fences, windows, etc.). Thus both the manual and automatic methods of matching similar features output a lot of good coordinates. However, my couch images have a more plain composition, with the gray topography of my friend's couch having less unique features to identify and correspond. You can see that the couch corner is more aligned in my automatic, RANSAC, mosaic in comparison to my manual mosaic.
